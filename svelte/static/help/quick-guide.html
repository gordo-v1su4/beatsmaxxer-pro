What makes an effect happen?

A trigger is a musical event that can start an effect. An onset means the start of a sound: a drum hit, a plucked note or a vocal syllable. Mix means the whole song.

Source / Channel
MIDI uses note starts. Audio sources use the whole song or an isolated stem. Choose Vocals to follow that part, or Channels by Deck to distribute available channels across the decks.
Threshold
A minimum trigger strength from 0 to 1. Raise it to ignore weaker events.
Density
Keep the strongest share in each channel. 40% keeps the loudest 40%. Lower density also adds spacing after each effect: (100% − Density) × 2 beats. It does not change clip speed.
Ramp Chance
40% gives eligible events a 40% chance to start a ramp. It does not shorten the ramp.
Gap
The minimum normal-playback time after an effect finishes, plus Density's extra spacing. 180 ms is 0.18 seconds; at 120 BPM and 65% Density, the added 0.7 beats makes the total gap 0.53 seconds. Triggers during the effect or total gap are ignored.
Global, Modular, Follow on Air and PGM

Global shares Timing parameters across the decks; each deck keeps its Speedramp/Stutter assignment. Modular edits the selected deck separately. Follow on Air makes the editor follow the deck currently on PGM; turn it off to keep editing one deck.

With MIDI or audio triggers, accepted effect bursts drive automatic PGM cuts. The upper random/interval/feel controls are inactive for those sources. A manual cut takes priority and resets the automatic cadence. The PGM trigger source instead starts an effect when that deck becomes the on-air video.

More about the audio sources

Mix onsets: attacks anywhere in the whole song. Vocals onsets: attacks in the isolated vocal track. Stem onsets: attacks in a selected part such as drums or bass. Vocals activity: vocal phrase entrances after quiet gaps. Mix loudness: broader loud peaks in the whole song. These come from audio analysis, while MIDI follows written notes.

Beats, bars and effect length

In this app’s 4/4 grid, 4 beats = 1 bar. Two beats is half a bar; two bars is eight beats. At 120 BPM, one beat lasts half a second.

Speedramp: Cycle is the curve’s length

2 bars runs one complete curve for eight beats after an accepted trigger. It then returns to normal playback. Another eligible trigger can start a new curve after the gap.

Fixed uses one length. Random chooses one of your four length choices for each accepted trigger.

Stutter: Division and repeats make a burst

1/8 is an eighth-note slice: half a beat. With Fixed / 3 repeats, that slice repeats three times, making a burst of one and a half beats.

By Energy chooses the burst from trigger strength. Stronger events produce more energetic repeat patterns. This control applies to Stutter, not Speedramp.

An effect finishes before another event can start it. Music triggers do not restart a ramp halfway through its curve.

Cycle, Every and Snap do different jobs

Cycle sets a Speedramp’s duration. Every, under the Beat trigger source, sets how often beat events are offered—for example every four beats. The effect still has to finish before another is accepted. Snap only aligns the points while you draw the speed curve.

Min × / Max × set the speed range: 0.5× is half speed, 1× is normal, and 2× is double speed. The curve shows how that speed changes over one cycle. Continuous cycles without waiting for note/onset triggers; density, chance and gaps do not apply.

Record it. Check it. Export it.

REC
Your on-air cuts are written to the song timeline, with the actual deck settings and clips captured for that take. A new pass replaces cuts under the playhead unless Overdub is on.
PLAY
The recorded cuts and section snapshots drive PGM. Clip-set edits are locked in REC and PLAY. Click the active mode again to return to the live workspace.
Save / Load
Download Arrangement saves the take, reusable clip sets and assignments. It does not embed your song or video files; keep those original files available.
Export
Choose the whole song or the selected loop, then the output frame rate. Prepared 96 FPS ramp media supplies smooth retiming; your exported video still uses the selected output rate, normally 24 FPS.
